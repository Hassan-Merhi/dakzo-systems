When custom software is worth it

Custom software is valuable when it removes a recurring operational constraint, not simply because a company wants something unique.

Signal 1: the same data is entered more than once

Repeatedly copying customer, inventory, order, shipment, or accounting data between systems creates delay and error risk. Integration may solve the problem; when the workflow spans several proprietary steps, a custom layer can become the cleaner solution.

Signal 2: spreadsheets have become operational infrastructure

Spreadsheets are excellent tools, but a file that controls approvals, inventory, production, payroll, dispatch, or customer status may need permissions, audit history, concurrency, validation, and reliable backups that a purpose-built application can provide.

Signal 3: staff work around the software

If people maintain parallel WhatsApp threads, notebooks, unofficial files, or manual checklists because the main system cannot represent the real workflow, the mismatch is measurable. Document those workarounds before deciding what to build.

Signal 4: the business needs one source of truth

Custom systems are especially useful when teams need shared state across locations or functions: inventory, production, delivery, customer service, finance, and management reporting. The goal is not one giant screen; it is consistent data and controlled transitions.

Do not build what you can buy well

Commodity problems such as basic email, payroll in a standard jurisdiction, or generic ticketing often have mature products. Custom development should focus on the processes where workflow fit, integration, control, or differentiation justify ownership.
